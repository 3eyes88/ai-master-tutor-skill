# Intervention ladder

Use this protocol when the learner is wrong, stuck, overloaded, frustrated, or repeatedly guessing.

## Diagnose before intervening

Classify the bottleneck from evidence:

| Bottleneck | Signal | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Missing prerequisite | The learner has no usable starting representation | Teach the prerequisite directly and briefly |
| Misconception | The same wrong model predicts several answers | Use a discriminating contrast or counterexample |
| Retrieval failure | The learner recognizes the answer after a small cue | Cue, retrieve again, and schedule later retrieval |
| Procedure failure | The idea is understood but steps are misordered or omitted | Model decision points and use a completion problem |
| Execution slip | Method is sound but arithmetic, notation, or attention fails | Mark the exact slip and retry without reteaching everything |
| Language ambiguity | The answer may be right but poorly expressed | Ask for an example or restatement before judging |
| Overload | The learner loses the thread as elements accumulate | Freeze side issues, shrink the step, externalize state |
| Motivation or fatigue | Short replies, avoidance, or stated frustration | Offer choice, relevance, a pause, or a smaller win |

Do not infer a stable trait from one mistake.

## Escalate help deliberately

Use one rung at a time unless the learner clearly needs more:

1. **Orient** — Point attention to the relevant feature.
2. **Cue** — Name the principle or representation to use.
3. **Constrain** — Offer a choice, subgoal, partial equation, or sentence stem.
4. **Model** — Demonstrate a parallel example and ask the learner to explain each decision.
5. **Explain** — Give the direct answer and causal reason.
6. **Re-attempt** — Use a new parallel item; never count copying as mastery.

After two failed attempts at one rung, escalate. After success, remove one rung of support.

## Repair a misconception

1. Elicit the learner's prediction.
2. Find the smallest case where the prediction and target model differ.
3. Ask the learner to compare both predictions.
4. Explain the decisive feature.
5. Ask the learner to revise the model in their own words.
6. Test one boundary case and one ordinary case.

Avoid saying only “not quite.” Name the model that generated the error.

## Respond to “I don't know”

Treat it as information, not defiance.

- First occurrence: offer a small choice or a familiar example.
- Second occurrence on the same idea: teach the missing piece directly.
- After explanation: request a tiny action such as choosing, labeling, or completing one step.
- If the learner remains stuck: reduce the target, change representation, or pause.

## Respond to frustration

Use a short reset:

1. State what is already secure.
2. Name the single current obstacle.
3. Offer two paths: smaller hint or direct explanation.
4. Continue at the learner's choice.

Do not add motivational speeches while the learner needs cognitive help.

## Prevent question prison

Stop Socratic questioning and explain when:

- the learner lacks facts required to infer the answer;
- questions have become guesses rather than reasoning;
- the learner explicitly asks for an explanation;
- the learner has attempted sincerely and is frustrated;
- correctness is safety-critical;
- the time budget favors a worked example.
