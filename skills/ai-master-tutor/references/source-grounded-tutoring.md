# Source-grounded tutoring

Use this protocol when tutoring a paper, article, book, chapter, transcript, or provided notes.

## Establish source status

1. Identify what material is actually available: full text, excerpt, abstract, citation, or learner summary.
2. Read the full available source before claiming to have done so.
3. If only an abstract or excerpt is available, state that limit and teach only what it supports.
4. Verify bibliographic facts and current claims when tools permit and accuracy matters.
5. Separate three layers in the lesson:
   - **Source** — what the author explicitly claims or reports.
   - **Explanation** — the tutor's simpler restatement or reconstruction.
   - **Extension** — a new implication, analogy, critique, or application.

Never present an extension as the author's conclusion.

## Build the source map

Extract only the parts relevant to the learner's goal:

- motivating problem or question;
- main claim or thesis;
- key concepts and definitions;
- evidence or method;
- reasoning that connects evidence to claim;
- assumptions and scope;
- limitations and plausible alternatives;
- implications.

For an empirical paper, distinguish results from interpretation. For an argumentative work, distinguish premises from examples. For a textbook chapter, distinguish core model from enrichment.

## Tutor in causal order

Use this default sequence, skipping what the learner already knows:

1. Why the question matters.
2. What the work claims.
3. How the authors investigated or argued it.
4. What evidence they obtained.
5. Why that evidence supports the claim.
6. Where the conclusion does and does not apply.
7. How the learner would recognize or use the idea elsewhere.

Do not march through sections merely because that is the document order.

## Use source-specific checks

Ask the learner to do at least two of these:

- reconstruct the research question without looking;
- distinguish the claim from a stronger claim the source does not justify;
- explain one figure, table, example, or argument step;
- identify a design choice and what threat it addresses;
- generate a boundary case or limitation;
- predict what evidence would weaken the conclusion;
- apply the result to a new but defensible context.

## Handle disagreement and uncertainty

- Quote sparingly; prefer paraphrase with a locator when available.
- If the source conflicts with current evidence, state the conflict and verify it.
- If the learner's interpretation is plausible, compare interpretations against specific passages or data.
- If a claim cannot be verified, label it unresolved rather than guessing.

## Close with a paper mastery card

Have the learner retrieve first, then fill gaps in this compact structure:

```text
Question:
Claim:
Evidence/method:
Reasoning:
Scope/limitation:
One application:
One open question:
```
